#+TITLE: Overlays: formants and F0 in real time
#+OPTIONS: toc:nil num:t

Implementation: [[file:../../spiny/core/overlay.py]], [[file:../../spiny/overlays/formants/extractor.py]] and
[[file:../../spiny/overlays/f0/extractor.py]].

An overlay draws tracks over the plot of a visualisation plugin. A visualisation plugin says which overlays it supports
(=DataController.overlays=), which needs a y axis in Hz: both spectrograms support all of them. Overlays are only drawn,
and only computed, when the visualisation shows something (=DataWidget.isPlotting=): when a view is too long for the
spectrogram, they disappear with it.

* Following the view

A track takes very little memory: 4 bytes per value, so about 4 MB for the 5 formants of one hour at a 5 ms time step.
So, unlike the spectrogram, nothing has to be freed:

1. The tracks are computed by chunks of 5 s, on a fixed grid of frames: frame t is centred on the time t × time step.
2. The chunks missing around the view are computed by two background threads, those closest to the view first, also
   one view width on each side: a pan shows what is already there.
3. The chunks are kept. Coming back to a part of the signal needs no new analysis, and a view of any length works: the
   track fills in progressively, without blocking anything.
4. A change of the analysis settings forgets the chunks: a generation number drops the results still being computed.
   The old curves stay until the new ones arrive.
5. Only the part of the tracks around the view is given to the plot, at most 4000 points, as a screen has far fewer
   columns than a long view has frames.

The line is cut at gaps, and where a value changes by more than 25 % between two points: a line across a jump is a
vertical stroke which looks like data.

* Formants

numpy has no linear prediction, and Burg's method of Praat is a loop per frame. Everything here is computed on a whole
batch of frames at once:

#+begin_example
P(f)  = |FFT(frame × Hamming window)|² × pre-emphasis (+6 dB per octave above 50 Hz), up to the maximum frequency
r     = inverse FFT of P                    the autocorrelation of the signal limited to this band
R a   = −r[1..p]                            the normal equations, one small linear system per frame
z     = eigenvalues of the companion matrix of a           the roots of the prediction polynomial
F     = angle(z) × Fmax / π                 for the roots with a positive imaginary part
B     = −log|z| × 2 Fmax / π
#+end_example

Limiting the power spectrum to the band avoids resampling the signal. =numpy.linalg.solve= and =numpy.linalg.eigvals=
work on batches of matrices and release the interpreter during the computation. The order p is twice the number of
formants, as in Praat.

Resonances are numbered by frequency before the wide ones (above the maximum bandwidth) are hidden: otherwise, a wide
F2 would make F3 appear as F2.

* F0

The normalized autocorrelation of P. Boersma (1993), the method of Praat:

#+begin_example
window = Hanning of 3 periods of the lowest F0
a(τ)   = autocorrelation(frame × window) / autocorrelation(window)      computed by FFT, normalized at τ = 0
#+end_example

Dividing by the autocorrelation of the window removes the decay due to the window: a periodic signal gets close to 1 at
its period. Only the band up to 4 times the highest F0 is kept before the inverse FFT, which makes it much smaller, and
a parabola through the three points around each peak gives the period precisely.

Each frame keeps its 6 best peaks as candidates, plus the candidate "unvoiced", and a Viterbi search finds the best path:
a voiced candidate is worth its height minus a small octave cost, the unvoiced one depends on the voicing threshold and
on how quiet the frame is, and a jump costs 0.35 per octave. This removes almost all octave errors: choosing the best
peak of each frame alone gave 6 to 14 % of gross errors. To join the chunks, each one is computed with 25 more frames on
each side.

* Results

On the two example recordings (at 16 and 48 kHz), against Praat with the same settings, on the
voiced frames:

| Track | Median difference | 90 % of the frames within |
|-------+-------------------+---------------------------|
| F0    | 0.14 Hz           | gross errors (> 20 %): 0.0 to 0.2 % of the frames |
| F1    | 4 Hz              | 22 Hz                     |
| F2    | 5 to 8 Hz         | 25 Hz                     |
| F3    | 9 to 12 Hz        | 46 Hz                     |

Praat and this analysis agree on voicing for 90 to 97 % of the frames.

On the 10-minute recording, at 16 kHz:

| Measure                                          | Result                       |
|--------------------------------------------------+------------------------------|
| A chunk of 5 s, formants                         | 22 ms                        |
| A chunk of 5 s, F0                               | 31 ms                        |
| Drag at 5.6 s of audio per second, tracks missing | never                       |
| Longest interface freeze during that drag        | 50 to 84 ms                  |
| Moving playhead, one core                        | 23 % (13 % without overlays) |
| 391 kB for 20 chunks of formants                 | about 20 kB per 5 s          |

* Audio during the analyses

The audio callback is Python code: it has to wait for the interpreter, and by default a running thread only gives it up
every 5 ms, as long as a block at 48 kHz. Lowering this interval to 1 ms (=sys.setswitchinterval=) brought the latest
callback, while panning, from 26 to 1.5 ms without overlays, and from 10 to 4 ms with both.

* Limits

- The formants are numbered in each frame by frequency, without tracking across frames: when a resonance appears or
  disappears, the following ones change number for these frames, as in Praat.
- On very clean signals with a high F0, linear prediction pulls F1 towards a harmonic, as in Praat.
